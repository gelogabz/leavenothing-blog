The first time you join a coastal cleanup, the scale is shocking. Not the amount of trash — the amount of *specific* trash. Bottles with labels from other countries. Flip-flops without pairs. Fishing line. Sachets. A toothbrush. A toy. A shoe.

You fill a sack, then another, and the beach looks almost the same. The tide keeps bringing more.

That is the lesson of the shoreline. It is not a bin. It is a boundary, and everything upstream eventually arrives.

## Clean is not the same as healthy

A cleanup is a good thing to do. It is also a stopgap. Removing plastic from a beach does not fix the production system that made it, the waste system that failed to collect it, or the storm that washed it out of a river.

A healthy coast is more than a clean coast. It has:

- **Vegetation at the edge.** Mangroves, beach forest, and dune grasses hold sediment, slow waves, and provide nursery habitat.
- **Room to move.** Shorelines shift. If every metre is built on, the coast has nowhere to go when the sea rises.
- **Clean water.** Sediment, sewage, and agricultural runoff can smother reefs and seagrass.
- **Intact reef and seagrass systems.** They break wave energy and support fisheries.
- **People who can still use it.** A coast that is fenced off for tourists is not the same as a coast that supports the community living behind it.

A beach can look pristine and still be ecologically broke. A beach can look messy — driftwood, seaweed, dunes — and be working exactly as it should.

## The tide as an archive

Low tide is the best time to see what the coast is doing. The wet sand holds tracks, channels, shells, and the small architecture of the intertidal zone. It also holds whatever the last high water left behind.

That is why the shoreline feels like a record. Every tide writes a new layer, and every layer includes us.

> The ocean does not distinguish between a bottle cap and a fish. It moves both.

## What actually helps

If you want to do more than fill a bag:

1. **Join a cleanup with a group that weighs and records what it collects.** Data is how cleanups become policy arguments.
2. **Reduce the disposable things you buy**, especially sachets and single-use bottles. This is unglamorous and more effective than a beach photo.
3. **Support mangrove and beach-forest restoration** led by local communities, not just tree-planting photo ops. Planting the wrong species in the wrong zone can do more harm than good.
4. **Ask where your waste goes.** In many coastal towns, the answer is "the sea" because collection is incomplete.
5. **Vote for sewage, drainage, and waste systems.** Infrastructure is conservation.

The shoreline will remember what we do. It will also remember what we fail to build.
