The name of this site is the rule. Leave nothing. Take only photographs. Leave only footprints. Sometimes not even those.

It is a familiar outdoor slogan, and like most familiar slogans it is easy to say and harder to practise. The photographs are not neutral. They change the places they depict. A geotag can send a thousand people to a fragile trail. A beautiful image can turn a nesting site into an attraction. A caption can flatten a complex place into a mood.

So this is a working ethic, not a certificate.

## The photograph is an intervention

Every photograph is a decision about what to include and what to leave out. The frame excludes the road, the rubbish, the crowd, the damage, the people who were asked to move. That is normal. Composition is selection.

The problem starts when we mistake the selection for the whole. A clean frame can make a place look healthier than it is. A dramatic frame can make a place look more remote than it is. A caption can make a place sound emptier than it is.

The antidote is not to stop photographing. It is to be honest about the frame.

- Say where and when, if it is safe to do so.
- Credit the people who helped you get there.
- Do not pretend a place is untouched when people live there.
- Do not use a photograph to sell a product or a politics it does not support.
- Do not share locations that cannot handle the attention.

## Slow is a technique

Most of the best photographs I have made were the result of waiting, not arriving. The light changed. The fog moved. A bird landed. Someone laughed. The frame I had planned was rarely the one that worked.

Waiting is also a form of respect. It gives the place time to stop reacting to you. It gives you time to notice what is actually happening rather than what you expected.

This is not a argument for technical perfection. It is an argument for attention. A sharp photograph of nothing is still a photograph of nothing.

> The camera is a reason to look. It should not become a reason to stop.

## The leave-no-trace version of photography

The practical rules are simple enough:

1. **Stay on established trails.** A tripod mark in a meadow is still a mark.
2. **Do not bait, call, or chase wildlife.** A photograph of a stressed animal is not a portrait.
3. **Do not fly drones where they disturb people or wildlife.** Check local rules.
4. **Ask before photographing people**, especially in their homes, farms, or ceremonies.
5. **Carry out everything.** Including the things that are "natural" but do not belong there.
6. **Do not geotag sensitive sites.** Share the region, not the exact coordinates.
7. **Support the place.** If you benefit from a landscape, contribute to the people and systems maintaining it.

None of this makes you a better photographer. It makes you a less harmful one. That is a lower bar and a more useful one.

## What remains

The places in these photographs will outlast the photographs. That is the correct order. The image is a note, not an achievement.

If I do this well, the site will be a record of attention: a ridge before the rain, a flower after it, a shoreline at low tide, a mountain doing nothing for anyone. The pictures are evidence that someone looked.

The rest is up to the place.
