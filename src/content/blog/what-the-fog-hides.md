From the road, a mountain forest can look like a green smudge. Nice enough, but empty. Then you walk into it and the scale changes. The air is colder. The ground is spongy. Every trunk is wearing moss, every branch is holding someone else's roots, and the fog is not weather so much as a delivery system.

In the Philippines, montane forests — the high, cool forests of the Cordillera, the Sierra Madre, Mindanao, and many smaller ranges — do a job that is easy to ignore because it happens slowly. They catch cloud. They strip water out of the air and let it drip into the soil. They hold that water, release it, and feed the streams that feed the lowlands.

The fog is infrastructure.

## A forest that drinks from the sky

Cloud forests are forests that live in the cloud belt. The trees are often stunted, twisted, and covered in moss, ferns, and orchids. That shaggy surface is not decoration. It increases the area that can intercept mist, and it slows rain down so the soil can absorb it instead of shedding it.

This matters because the Philippines gets its water in bursts. Typhoons, monsoons, long dry spells. A healthy montane forest acts like a sponge with a memory: it holds what falls, and it keeps releasing it after the rain has stopped. Remove the forest and the same rain arrives faster, hits harder, and leaves sooner. The streams run high and brown, then low and clear, then not at all.

## The neighbourhood

The forest is also a neighbourhood. Birds move seed. Bats pollinate. Insects break down the dead wood that becomes the next generation of soil. Frogs and lizards and rats and civets and snakes and things without common names all live in the layers of a forest that looks, from a distance, like one green texture.

You do not see most of them. That is the point. A forest is not a collection of individual trees; it is the relationships between them, plus the water, the fungus, the light, and the time.

> A forest is not a view. It is a system that happens to be visible.

## What the fog is hiding

The usual story about Philippine forests is about loss. That story is real: logging, land conversion, mining, fires, and roads have taken a lot. But the more useful story is about what remains and why it matters.

Montane forests protect watersheds. They reduce landslide risk. They keep carbon out of the atmosphere. They give species somewhere to move as the lowlands heat up. They are also places where communities have lived and managed land for generations, often with better long-term results than the agencies that replaced them.

The fog hides all of this because it is not dramatic. There is no single villain in a cloud. There is only the slow, unglamorous work of keeping a watershed intact.

## What to do with this

If you hike in these mountains, the practical ethic is simple: stay on the trail, pack out everything, do not pick the moss, do not fly a drone over nesting season, and do not geotag a fragile site to a public feed.

If you live downstream, the connection is direct. The water in your tap and the water in the forest are the same water. The trees are not a backdrop to the city. They are part of its plumbing.

If you want to go further, follow the work of groups mapping watersheds and forest cover, and support the people who are already doing the long, boring, necessary work. The fog will keep hiding it. We can still choose to look.
