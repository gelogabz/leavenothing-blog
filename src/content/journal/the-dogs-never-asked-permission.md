They were not unhappy in the city. That is the part I keep having to correct in my head.

They had a routine, and the routine was fine. Two walks a day, both of them on the same loop, both of them on concrete. They knew the route so well that they would slow down at the corner where the neighbour's cat sat on a wall, and speed up at the stretch where a bakery put its trays out. They slept a great deal. They were loved in the way city pets are loved: carefully, indoors, on a schedule.

What they did not have was anywhere to go.

## A city dog is a dog on a timetable

In a city, a dog's day is made of decisions other people already made. Where the walk goes. When it happens. How long it lasts. Whether the ground is hot. Whether the ground is allowed at all. The whole environment is either private property or a road, and a dog on a leash is a guest in both.

The clever thing about dogs is that they accept this completely. They build a life inside the small space and they enjoy it. Ours learned the sound of the lift, the sound of the gate, the exact moment when the lead came off the hook. Everything was fine.

But watch a dog in a city and then watch a dog on a shore, and the difference is not happiness. It is permission.

## The first thing they did was stand in the water

There is a photograph I took of a dog in the shallows on Siquijor, standing still in water up to its knees, a long way from anybody. I did not tell it to do that. Nothing was there. It just went, and stopped, and stayed.

Our two did something similar, and it was not graceful. The sea is loud, and the first wave is a shock. One of them barked at it. The other one ran away from it sideways and then came back to check whether it had followed. For about ten minutes the sea was an emergency.

Then it stopped being an emergency, and became a fact. Water arrives, water leaves. There is no alarm in that, and nothing to guard. Most of what a city dog spends its life reacting to is other people's rules. The tide is not a rule. It is just weather, and after a while they treated it the way the janitor treats a corridor: passing through, unconcerned.

## What a long beach does to a personality

The change I noticed was not energy. It was attention span.

In the city, a walk is a hundred interruptions. A scooter, a smell, a gate, another dog, a child, a sudden change of direction on the lead. In that world, a dog that is not constantly scanning is a dog about to be run over. So they scanned. Constantly, and at eye level.

On a shore, there is nothing to scan. The horizon does not move. So they went low instead. They put their heads down and read the beach the way you would read a long letter — every footprint, every bit of weed, every bird that had stood there earlier. These are the same animals. They just stopped watching the traffic and started reading the mail.

Then the tiredness changed. A city walk ends with a dog that is exercised. A beach walk ends with a dog that has used its whole brain for an hour and is now asleep on its side under a bench, wet, sand in its eyebrows, gone.

## The dogs who were already free

Not every dog here lives inside a house. Most of them don't. They have names and nobody's on the paperwork. They stand in the shallows on hot afternoons because the water is cooler than the road. They walk the low tide flats because there is food out there. They sleep in the shadow of a boat and they move when they like.

I do not want to pretend that this is a paradise. A free dog in a Philippine town lives a shorter and harder life than a dog in an air-conditioned flat, and it meets the road, and the road wins. That is real.

But it is honest about something my two were not learning. Out here a dog is treated less as property and more as a neighbour. It belongs to the street rather than to a person. People feed it because it is there and it is thin. It sleeps where it wants.

When I photograph the loose dogs — the one standing alone in shallow water, the tan one crossing an enormous low tide with the mountains behind it — I am photographing an animal that has never once been told to wait.

## What they still don't understand

They still do not understand the point of rain. They still think the loudest thing in the world is a motorbike, which was true in the city and is also true here. They still stop at the edge of the water when the swell is big, and look at me to check whether this is allowed. That look is the last of the city, and it is fading slowly, and I am in no hurry for it to go.

What they have learned instead is that the outside is not a corridor between the indoors and the indoors. It is a place with a floor you can dig, a wind you have to turn your back to, water you can walk into, and enough room to run without anyone calling you back.

## The point

We got dogs and then we moved, and it's easy to dress that up as a gift to them. It was mostly a gift to me. But I do think this is the most useful thing anyone has done for these two: not more love, not better food — a horizon.

Nobody taught them to be free. Both times we walked out onto the sand they simply checked, the way any sensible animal would, whether the lead was still attached. It wasn't, and out they went, and neither of them looked back to ask permission.

Whatever we are to them, we are now something other than the people who decide where the walk goes. That took moving a long way from a corner where a cat sat on a wall.

They have never once said thank you. They don't have to.
