You have to look up to understand the National Museum of Natural History. The facade is formal and symmetrical, the kind of building that asks you to climb a short flight of steps and behave. Then you step into the courtyard, tip your head back, and the roof turns into a forest.

Steel branches fan out from a central trunk. Glass triangles sit between them like leaves that forgot to fall. Beyond the glass is Manila sky, pale blue and moving. The structure is called the Tree of Life, and it is doing the oldest job a tree does: holding up a canopy, letting light through, and making a place underneath feel sheltered.

## A building that kept changing its mind

The shell around that dome has had several lives. It was built in 1940 as the Agriculture and Commerce Building, designed in the neoclassical style by Antonio Toledo in the late 1930s. It is the twin of the Finance Building across Agrifina Circle, same dimensions, same floor plan, a matched pair of government buildings laid out around a roundabout in Rizal Park.

Then the war arrived. The building was destroyed in the Battle of Manila in 1945, along with most of the city around it, and rebuilt afterwards to Toledo's original plans. It later became the Department of Tourism. In 2013 it began another conversion, this time into a museum of natural history, with Dominic Galicia Architects and interior designer Tina Periquet leading the adaptive reuse. It opened to the public in May 2018.

That sequence is worth sitting with. Agriculture and commerce, then tourism, then natural history. The same neoclassical box has held three different ideas about what the Philippines is for. The building did not change. The country's understanding of its own resources did.

## The canopy is the argument

The renovation could have stopped at restoration. Instead it cut a courtyard open to the sky and closed it again with a 40-metre glass-and-steel dome, engineered by Nippon Steel, supported by a double-helix trunk that also houses a lift. The form borrows from DNA as much as from trees, which is appropriate for a museum whose collection is the evidence of evolution.

Outside, the building still speaks the language of columns, pediments, and symmetry. Inside, it speaks the language of branching, light, and growth. The two are not in conflict. They are a sentence and its correction.

Natural history museums tend to be built like banks: heavy, sealed, protective. This one is built like a canopy. It argues that the collection is not a warehouse of specimens but a living system, and that the building should admit the weather it is describing. Standing under the dome, you are inside and outside at once. The sky is part of the exhibit.

## An arena under a heavy sky

In Sorsogon City, a day's drive south of Manila, another building is also quoting the past. The Sorsogon Sports Arena in Barangay Balogo is a four-storey curve of white arches, patterned after the Colosseum in Rome. It seats around 12,000 people, can stretch to 20,000 for big events, and was inaugurated in October 2024 as the centrepiece of the provincial sports complex.

From the ground, the resemblance is obvious: tiers of arches, columns between them, a facade that wraps around an oval instead of ending in a corner. The arches are structural, but they are also a shade device. They open the building to air, cast deep shadows, and give athletes and spectators somewhere to stand out of the rain without going inside. In a country that gets hit by typhoons, that is not decoration. It is climate control by geometry.

The photograph I keep returning to is mostly cloud. The arena occupies the lower third of the frame, a thin white arc holding its ground while the sky builds above it. That is the honest proportion. A building in the Philippines is always a small thing under a large atmosphere.

## Every building is a climate argument

The Colosseum itself is a ruin now. Stone seats, broken arches, grass growing where the emperor used to sit. Nature did not need permission to move back in. The Sorsogon arena is new, but it is already weathering: rain streaks the white concrete, sun bleaches it, salt in the air finds the joints. In twenty years it will look different. In two hundred, if it lasts, it will look like the thing it copied.

That is not a criticism. It is the point. Architecture is never separate from ecology. The steel in the museum dome was mined, smelted, and shipped. The concrete in the arena was mixed from sand and aggregate, probably dug from a river somewhere in Bicol. The neoclassical facades of Manila sit on land that was once swamp and shoreline, filled and reshaped until the city could stand there. History is natural history with paperwork.

What changes is whether a building works with the weather or against it. The old tropical grammar — deep eaves, shaded arcades, courtyards, cross-ventilation, raised floors — was developed before air conditioning and is still the most reliable technology we have. The museum's new dome borrows the logic of a tree. The arena borrows the logic of a Roman arcade. Both are old solutions to an old problem: how to stand under the sky without being beaten by it.

So I look up. At the museum, the geometry is delicate and bright, a canopy that lets the sky through. At the arena, it is heavy and repetitive, a wall of arches holding a crowd. Two buildings, two centuries, one climate. The trees were doing this long before either of them.
