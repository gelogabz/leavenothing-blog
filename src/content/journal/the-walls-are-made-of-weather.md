Every old church in the Philippines is built out of old weather. The coral in the walls grew in a reef. The volcanic rock came out of a mountain. The brick was river clay, fired in a kiln. The mortar, in at least one town, is said to be lime mixed with egg white and tuba, the coconut wine sold in the market. None of it is abstract. A church is a sample of the ground it stands on, rearranged into a room.

I did not set out to photograph churches. I set out to photograph buildings that had been outside long enough to show what outside does to them. After a few years, the churches were the only ones left in the folder.

## A reef that stopped growing

The Basilica del Santo Niño in Cebu is the oldest church in the country, founded in April 1565, the month Legazpi's expedition arrived. The building standing now is at least the fourth on that ground. The first two were wood and nipa, and they burned. The present one was finished in 1740 from hewn stone, much of it limestone quarried across the channel on Mactan.

That is the pattern for everything that follows. The site is older than the building. The building is older than the idea that it is permanent.

In 2013, an earthquake in Bohol shook Cebu hard enough to bring down the basilica's limestone bell tower. The church reopened on Christmas Eve the following year. You can read that as resilience or as maintenance. I read it as weather: the same material that made the wall beautiful made it heavy, and the ground eventually had an opinion.

In Sorsogon, on the Pacific side, Barcelona Church takes the idea further. It was built in 1874 from coral blocks cut out of the sea and stacked into walls a metre and a half thick. Local accounts say the mortar was lime, egg white, and tuba. The belfry faces the water and doubled as a watchtower. The church is not merely beside the sea. It is made of the sea, and it is pointed at it.

## Brick is a different argument

Alcala, in Cagayan, is brick. The clay came out of the Cagayan River valley and was fired in kilns near the water, and in 1881 the town stacked the result into a church twenty-four metres wide, with four rows of pews instead of the usual two. People call it the widest church in the country.

Brick is a different kind of material from coral or volcanic rock. Stone is found; brick is made. A brick is small, uniform, and repeatable, which lets a town raise a broad, even wall without a quarry or a volcano. It also records labour in a way stone does not: every brick was cut, dried, fired, carried, and laid. The facade is a ledger.

## Built on the thing that destroyed the last one

Daraga is the photograph I keep returning to. The church sits on a hill above the town, its facade carved from volcanic rock, and Mayon fills the right side of the frame. On the day I took it, the volcano was half under cloud and the stone was almost black with rain.

The Franciscans built the church in 1773. Forty-one years later, Mayon erupted and buried the church at Cagsawa, a few kilometres away; only its bell tower stayed above the ash. People assume Daraga was built because of that. It wasn't. The church on the hill was already middle-aged when the other one disappeared.

So the building is both refuge and warning, and it is made of the same material that did the burying. The walls are volcanic rock. The ornament is volcanic rock. In 2007 the National Museum declared the facade, belfry, and baptistry a National Cultural Treasure. The lime wash that holds the surface together is renewed the way you would repaint a boat.

There is no separation between the architecture and the geology here. The church is the volcano, rearranged into a nave.

## The belfry outlived the church

In San Juan, on Siquijor, the parish dates to 1863. The Spanish-era church was ruined by an earthquake in 1897. What remains is the coral-stone belfry: dark, octagonal, arched, still standing at the edge of the yard. Beside it, the modern church is pale concrete with a gable and a rose window, built to replace what the ground took.

I like this pair more than any single building. The old tower is a ruin that was never cleared away, and the new church is a replacement that did not pretend to be old. Two centuries of decisions stand a few metres apart.

A short drive away, in Siquijor town, the Sto. Niño church is white with black trim and iron grilles, its gable curling up into a small bell turret. It looks like a chapel built by a community that had paint and welding rods and not much else, and it is exact in a way that grander churches are not. Small-town churches are honest about their budgets.

## Small buildings, long memory

Gubat, in Sorsogon, has a church with an 18th-century foundation and a much later skin. The town's own account says that in 1768 every man was asked to contribute a cubic metre of coral stone, and that it took ten years before construction could start. The church has been rebuilt since; the foundation is the original part. At Christmas the whole facade is outlined in white lights, and tricycles park along the road in front. On the night I was there, the building was less architecture than lantern.

Guinobatan, in Albay, tells the same story with a different accent. The parish goes back to the 1660s. The present church was inaugurated in December 1906, after eruptions, fires, and war had taken the earlier ones. It has a dark red dome and a cream facade streaked with rain, and it is best seen from underneath, through a tree.

And then there is the Little Chapel at FarmPlate, in Daraga: a small wooden building with a pointed steeple and Gothic windows, built recently as part of a farm park and often billed as the smallest chapel in the country. It has no congregation and no parish. It is scenery, and it is also a quiet room where people lower their voices. Both things are true at once.

## What the walls are arguing

A church in this country is a climate argument that has been running for four hundred years. Thick walls slow the heat. High windows pull light in and let air out. A belfry doubles as a watchtower. A hill keeps the town's flood away. Local material keeps the cost within reach. None of this was decoration. It was survival, dressed as ornament.

The materials have changed — coral to concrete, lime to cement, wood to steel — but the argument has not. How do you stand under this sky without being beaten by it? Every building in these photographs is an answer, and none of them is final.

I photograph churches because they are the only buildings in a Philippine town old enough to have been tested. A house gets replaced. A mall gets renovated. A church gets rebuilt on the same foundation, by the same town, in whatever material the decade can afford. That makes it a record: of geology, of labour, of weather, and of the stubborn decision to keep putting a roof over the same patch of ground.

The sermon ends. The weather does not.
