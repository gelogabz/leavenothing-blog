Mayon has a shape that does most of the work for you. The cone is almost geometric. Put it behind a beach, a coconut palm, a laundry line, a dog — it still reads. This is why it shows up in so many photographs, including some of mine.

But a mountain is not a backdrop. It is a volcano, a watershed, a hazard zone, a farm, a weather system, and a very large piece of geology that does not care whether the light is good.

## The photograph is the smallest part

The best-known view of Mayon is from the plains around Legazpi, where the cone rises out of the haze. It is spectacular in the way a postcard is spectacular: immediate, legible, and easy to consume.

The less photogenic story is the one that matters. Mayon is one of the most active volcanoes in the Philippines. Its slopes are farmed. Its base is densely settled. Its eruptions force evacuations. The permanent danger zone is not a suggestion.

When a place becomes an image, it is easy to forget that it is also a place where people live. The photograph flattens the risk. The cone looks permanent. The farms look picturesque. The evacuation centres are out of frame.

## Travel lightly, and listen

There is a version of hiking photography that treats the mountain as a stage and the people on it as extras. I have made those pictures too. They are not evil, but they are incomplete.

A better practice is slower:

- Ask before photographing people, especially in their homes or fields.
- Do not fly drones over wildlife, villages, or eruption zones without permission.
- Respect closures. A trail closed for rehabilitation is not a challenge.
- Do not geotag fragile sites, nesting areas, or places that are already overrun.
- Carry out everything you carry in, including the things you thought were biodegradable.

The last one is not a metaphor. Orange peels, apple cores, and "compostable" wrappers do not belong in a forest that is not your garden.

## The mountain does not need a caption

There is a particular kind of outdoor content that turns every view into a personal achievement: summit reached, weather conquered, light captured. It is understandable. Hiking is hard, and the view feels earned.

But the mountain was doing something long before you arrived and will keep doing it after you leave. The photograph is a record of a brief meeting. It is not the point of the meeting.

The point is to notice what is actually there: the volcanic soil, the orchids on the lava, the farmers who know which slope is safe this month, the birds that arrive after the rain. The point is to leave the place as capable of continuing as you found it.

Mayon does not need us to make it beautiful. It needs us to stop treating it as scenery.
